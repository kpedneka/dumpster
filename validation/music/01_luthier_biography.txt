Ines Thackeray trained as a cabinetmaker before turning, in her later years, to instrument building. Her early work followed conventional guitar designs closely, but she began experimenting with resonator chambers borrowed from cabinet-making, hollowing box-shaped bodies with internal baffles meant to project sound further than a traditional soundhole allowed.

After years of prototypes she reportedly burned rather than sold when they failed to satisfy her, Thackeray completed an instrument she called the Thackeray Resonator: a twelve-string, box-bodied instrument played upright and plucked, with a distinctive baffle system that gave it a louder, more sustained tone than comparable instruments of the period. She built only a small number of examples, selling most through a single shop and giving several away to touring musicians she admired.

Thackeray kept no formal workshop records. She never trained an apprentice, and the baffle technique she developed for the Resonator's body was never fully documented outside her own hands. A local historical society holds two surviving examples of her work, but no complete set of building plans has ever been recovered.
